The ferry to Harrow Island left the mainland twice a day, and on the morning Ines Marlowe took it there were only four passengers: herself, a man with a crate of seedlings, and two children who had been told to sit still and were not managing it. The sea was flat and grey. Gulls followed the boat for the first mile and then lost interest, the way gulls do when nobody throws anything.

She had been hired to repair the island's clock. That was all the letter said, in a careful hand, signed by someone called Aldous Penn on behalf of the parish council: the clock in the square had stopped in the spring, nobody on the island knew how to mend it, and they would pay her fare, her lodging, and a fee that was modest but not insulting. Ines had mended clocks for nineteen years. She had learned the trade from her aunt, who kept a shop no wider than a corridor, and she had learned from the same aunt that a stopped clock is rarely a broken one. More often something small has drifted out of place, and the whole mechanism, being honest, refuses to pretend otherwise.

The square was easy to find because the island had only one. The clock stood on a short stone tower above the fish market, and its hands pointed to seventeen minutes past three. Underneath it, as if the hour had been agreed on by everybody, the market was closed and the shutters were down.

"It is not three," said the man with the seedlings, who had walked up from the harbour behind her. "It is not even ten. But people here have got used to it. My neighbour says he likes knowing that at least one thing on this island is always the same."

"And you?"

"I grow tomatoes," he said. "Tomatoes do not care what the clock says, but the boat does, and I have missed it twice."

Aldous Penn turned out to be a tall, apologetic man who kept the key to the tower on a string around his neck. He led her up forty-one steps, counted them aloud out of habit, and opened a door that had swollen in its frame. The room behind it smelled of dust, oil and old rope. The movement sat in the middle of the floor in its iron cradle, larger than she had expected and in better condition. Somebody had loved it once. The brass was dull, but there was no rust on the arbors, and the weights hung straight.

She did not touch anything for the first hour. She sat on an upturned bucket and looked. This was the part of the work that customers never understood and never wanted to pay for, and it was the part that mattered. A clock is an argument made of wheels: each one says something to the next, and when the conversation stops, the place where it stopped is usually visible to anyone patient enough to follow the argument from the beginning. She followed it from the weight to the great wheel, from the great wheel to the centre wheel, and so on up the train, and everything answered as it should, until she reached the escapement and found a sparrow's nest.

It was an old nest, long abandoned, wedged between the pallets and the frame with a craftsmanship she could only respect. The birds had come in through a gap under the eaves, found a warm and regular machine, and built their house in the one place that would stop it. Ines laughed, which made Aldous Penn look worried.

"Is it bad?" he asked.

"It is the best kind of bad," she said. "Nothing is worn, nothing is bent. Your clock was asked to choose between keeping time and crushing a nest, and it stopped. I will need a small brush, a bowl of warm water, and about two days, because while I am here I may as well clean the rest."

On the second evening she set the hands by her own watch, gave the pendulum a push with one finger, and listened. The beat was even. Down in the square a shutter went up, then another. The man with the seedlings stood by the fountain looking at the tower as if it had spoken to him, and perhaps, in its way, it had. The next morning she was on the early ferry, and for once it left exactly on time.
